# Design brief — Personal Portfolio, Round 4: Lego-inspired directions

You are continuing early design work on a personal portfolio website. No code has been written yet; this phase is about choosing a visual direction. Everything you need is in this brief.

## The task

Design **3–4 Lego-inspired branding directions** for the site, as artboards on a design canvas, so Carson can compare them and pick one.

Add them as a new page, **"Round 4 — Lego"**, on the existing canvas:
https://claude.ai/artifact/ETUpymLMHC5A2ftgWJCRQz
(Rounds 1–3 are already there; read the canvas first and keep everything on it intact.) If you cannot add to that canvas, make a new one and say so.

Each artboard should be **720 × 1240 px** and follow the same layout as the earlier rounds, so they compare side by side:

1. A header strip with the direction's letter and name (continue from **J** onward).
2. Two or three sentences on the concept.
3. The wordmark / logo treatment.
4. The colour palette, with hex values.
5. The type system: display face and body face, with a sample of each.
6. A homepage hero shown in a mock browser frame: nav, headline, one-line intro, and entry points to the main sections.

In your reply (not on the artboards), give each direction an honest one-line tradeoff and recommend one.

## Who this is for

**Carson Jones** is a student at the University of South Carolina studying finance and supply chain. He has recently been spending much more time on software development and wants the site to showcase it. He isn't sure yet what career he wants, and he enjoys building software.

The site has two jobs:
- **An online résumé** that brings résumé bullet points to life. It will be pasted into the link field on job applications, so **recruiters are a primary audience**.
- **A showcase of his software-building ability**, through thoughtful interactive design.

It will also be linked from his Instagram bio, so **expect most visitors on phones**, arriving with no context. Designs must work at phone width.

## The brand thesis (settled)

> **Carson notices gaps and closes them.**

Everything he has built started from something missing. The tool changes (a spreadsheet, a business, a community, a Supabase backend), but the instinct doesn't. Finance and supply chain are the reason he thinks in systems, not a detour from software. Lego fits this thesis naturally: every venture and every build is a brick, and the body of work is something assembled piece by piece.

## Content the site will hold

**Ventures and leadership** (each gets its own subpage):
- **Run With Christ Columbia**: a faith-based run club he founded in Columbia, SC. He describes it as a movement to "make Heaven more full," a community built around faith. It is his favourite thing he does as a student. Keep it prominent, and describe it in terms of what he built (community, consistency, getting people to show up) so it reads well to both audiences.
- **Bridge Street Health**: his parents' direct primary care startup. He helps with operations and finances.
- **A mobile car detailing business** he started.
- **A sneaker reselling business** he started.

**Software builds** (shown on an interactive timeline):
- A portfolio project for a capstone program he applied to
- Google Apps Scripts, increasingly sophisticated
- A website built with HTML
- A feedback form using Supabase and free hosting
- This portfolio site itself, which he will build while learning
- More to come

Each timeline entry needs an expandable section covering **why he built it this way and what the missing piece was**, easy to find at a glance.

**Writing**: a blog of devotionals, reflections, and anything else, with a **tag system**.

**About**: short, with one simple portrait. Little of his face elsewhere; imagery is mostly **screenshots and environmental photographs**.

**Domain**: some variation of his name (earlier mocks used `carsonjones.com` as a placeholder).

## Site structure (settled)

One clean landing page that works as a preview of every section, **not a fork** ("recruiters click here, friends click there"). The homepage shows a small real slice of each section (a couple of timeline entries, a blog excerpt, a venture), and each clicks through to the full section. Detail builds up as the reader goes deeper; the important things are visible at a glance.

Main sections: **Timeline, Ventures, Writing, About**.

## What has been tried, and the feedback

- **Round 1: five concepts** (a ledger/finance notebook, a bold "gap" motif, a dark supply-chain network diagram, a warm Columbia-photography direction, a tagged card index). Carson's verdict: none of these.
- **Round 2: Super Mario homage**, four directions ranging from literal pixel art to an instruction-booklet style. He liked **G, "primaries"** (only Mario's red, blue, yellow and green, in a clean grid), but said it **did not look nearly professional enough**.
- **Round 3: G, made professional.** Deepened the primaries (crimson #A62B25, navy #1B4E8F, ochre #C99A2E, forest #2E7D4F), reduced colour to bars, squares and dots on neutral backgrounds, and used sober typefaces. G3 was recommended: colour as a system, where each colour means one kind of work (red ventures, blue software, gold writing, green leadership) and follows it across timeline, tags and filters.
- He then wanted to explore **Lego** as a theme that ties to the "builder" idea. That is this round.

**The most important preference he stated:** he is **unwilling to sacrifice professionalism for a theme or colour scheme he likes**. Every Lego direction must look credible to a recruiter first. Treat the theme as a signature, not a costume. It is fine for directions to range from subtle to more literal, but none should look like a toy or a kids' site. Round 3's lessons apply: restrained colour, neutral ground, serious type.

## Hard constraints

- **Lego intellectual property.** Do not use the LEGO name or logo, minifigure shapes, official brick artwork, or LEGO's exact red wordmark style. Use generic ideas: studs, bricks, plates, stacking, snapping together, building-instruction pages (step numbers, exploded views, parts lists). The site goes on job applications; a takedown would be damaging.
- **No invented facts.** Don't make up dates, numbers, metrics, client names or quotes. Use visible placeholders like `[YEAR]` or `[PHOTO: …]`. Known counts you can use: **4 ventures, 5 software builds**. The chronological order of his ventures is **unknown**, so don't imply one. The one-line "what was missing" descriptions need his own words; mark any you write as placeholders.
- **No emoji.** Draw icons as simple inline SVG.
- **Mobile.** The homepage hero should obviously translate to a phone.
- **Accessibility.** Text contrast of at least 4.5:1; don't put body text in light colours on white.

## How Carson likes to work

- He is **not picky and relies on the designer's expertise**, but wants **concrete visual options to react to** rather than being asked to describe a vibe. Give a clear recommendation.
- He has **limited coding experience** and treats this project as a way to learn. When you explain a design choice, use plain language and briefly say why it works.
- It's a **back-burner project** with no deadline, worked on a couple of hours at a time.
- Keep replies short: what was made, the tradeoffs, your recommendation, what you need from him.

## Ideas worth exploring (optional)

- The timeline as a stack of bricks, each brick one build or venture, colour-coded by kind of work (compatible with Round 3's G3 system).
- A building-instruction-booklet feel: numbered steps, parts lists as a "what I used" list for each project (tools, stack).
- A stud grid used quietly as texture or as a layout grid.
- Bricks that "snap" into place as a restrained scroll or hover interaction on the timeline.

## After this round

Once Carson picks a direction, the next step is a written project brief: site map, page-by-page content plan, the interactions worth building, and a staged build order he can learn from. Don't start that until he has chosen.
